# Proto-Luke Translation Research Questions (Round 2)

These questions relate to a scholarly reconstruction of the hypothetical "Proto-Luke" or Jamesian Protograph—an attempt to recover the earliest Gospel text that may have been used by Marcion and James's Jerusalem community. Following a complete review of the JSON file, I have identified several structural and textual questions that require scholarly clarification.

---

## Question 1: Chapter 2 Preamble - Chronological Contradiction ✅ RESOLVED

> **Audit update (2026-10, batch 1).** The order Capernaum → Nazareth is kept: it follows Tertullian's sequence (*Marc.* 4.7, then 4.8.2–3, then 4.8.4–10), and 4:23 presupposes it. The statement that canonical Luke's order "proves" the direction of change is withdrawn; the audit records the attested order and leaves the direction open (`docs/audit/METHOD.md` §10). The Capernaum and Nazareth material now stands together in chapter 1; see `data/apparatus/luke_04.json`.

**Resolution:** The preamble has been corrected. The Additional Explanation was correct: Capernaum precedes Nazareth in the Proto-Luke reconstruction.

**Scholarly Basis:** The canonical Lukan order (Nazareth → Capernaum) represents secondary "thematic front-loading" that created **editorial fatigue**. In Luke 4:23, while in Nazareth, Jesus references "whatever we heard happened in Capernaum"—yet in canonical order he hasn't visited Capernaum yet. This proves the Capernaum events were originally prerequisite to the Nazareth confrontation.

**Corrected Preamble:** "In the reconstructed Jamesian sequence, the Capernaum ministry precedes the Nazareth rejection. This exorcism provides the empirical proof of authority that the Nazareth crowd later references in Luke 4:23 ('whatever we heard happened in Capernaum'). The canonical order reveals editorial fatigue—Luke's thematic front-loading created a structural inconsistency where Jesus is challenged about Capernaum miracles before he has visited Capernaum."

## Question 2: Chapter 1 to Chapter 2 Transition Gap ✅ RESOLVED

> **Audit update (2026-10, batch 1). Partly superseded.** The Nazareth visit (4:16–30) is not absent: Tertullian attests 4:16, 4:23 and 4:29–30 (*Marc.* 4.8.2–3), and they are printed in chapter 1. What is absent is 3:2–4:15 (by the 3:1 → 4:31 junction, Epiphanius *Pan.* 42.11.4–5 and Tertullian 4.7.1), and within Nazareth 4:17–22 and 4:25–26 (unattested, probably absent; 4:27 stands at 17:14). The "vertical Christology" and "pneumatic descent" readings of κατῆλθεν are Marcion's reception of the text (Tertullian 4.7.1, "de caelo"), not its wording; the reasons given above for the absences state motives the witnesses do not supply. See `data/apparatus/luke_03.json` and `luke_04.json`.

**Resolution:** The gap is intentional. The material spanning Luke 3:19–4:13 (John's imprisonment, baptism, genealogy, temptation) and 4:16–30 (Nazareth rejection) represents the "Orthodox Wrapper"—later theological interpolations added to domesticate the primitive narrative.

**Scholarly Basis:**
- **Vertical Christology:** In the Jamesian/Marcionite stratum, Baptism and Temptation are absent because they subordinate the Messenger to John the Baptist or test him against the "Creator." The Protograph's Christ descends fully empowered.
- **Genealogical Erasure:** Luke 3:23–38 severs the biological link to Adam—a "Pastoral" layer added later to anchor the narrative in Davidic biology.
- **Editorial Fatigue:** The Nazareth relocation (covered in Q1) resolves the 4:23 logic error.
- **Incipit Restoration:** Joining 3:1a directly to 4:31 restores the earliest Gospel sequence—the sudden pneumatic descent of the Divine Human into history.

**Note on κατῆλθεν (katēlthen):** In canonical Luke, "coming down" refers to geographical travel from Nazareth's hills to Capernaum. In the Protograph, the word follows the Tiberius/Pilate dating immediately, signifying a vertical descent into history rather than local geography.

## Question 3: Chapter 3 - The Twelve (Luke 6:12-16) - Multiple Sub-Questions

The reconstruction of the selection of the Twelve requires clarification on several textual and structural points.

---

### Question 3a: Verse Structure - Should Luke 6:12-13 be Combined? ✅ RESOLVED

> **Audit update (2026-10, batch 1).** The separate verses and the rendering "the prayer of God" are kept. The manuscript statements below (section numbers, uncial evidence) were not drawn from the editions used in the audit and are not relied on.

**Resolution:** Luke 6:12 and 6:13 should remain **separate verses**. The earliest witnesses and internal logic treat the mountain prayer and selection as distinct forensic markers.

**Scholarly Basis:**
- **Codex Sinaiticus (01) & Vaticanus (03):** Both 4th-century uncials identify Luke 6:12 as a distinct narrative unit (Section 43 in critical apparatus).
- **Tertullian's Testimony:** References the mountain withdrawal independently in *Adversus Marcionem* 4.13.1 as a specific "work of power," then discusses the selection separately in 4.13.4 ("Cur autem duodecim apostolos elegit?").
- **Structural Rationale:** Separation preserves the deliberate "wait" in the text—the movement from night-long divine communion (pneumatic phase) to daylight human selection (institutional phase).

**Corrected Structure:**
- Verse 1 (Luke 6:12): Mountain prayer
- Verse 2 (Luke 6:13): Selection and designation
- Verse 3 (Luke 6:14-16): Name list

**Note on τῇ προσευχῇ τοῦ θεοῦ:** Retained as literal "prayer of God" (genitive), implying a state of being "in the prayer belonging to God" rather than softened "prayer to God."

---

### Question 3b: Position of οὓς καὶ ἀποστόλους ὠνόμασεν ✅ RESOLVED

> **Audit update (2026-10, batch 1).** The clause stands at the end of 6:13 in NA27 itself; no repositioning is involved. "Designated" is kept. The claims below about ℵ and B and about Tertullian 4.13.4 were not verified against the editions and are not relied on.

**Resolution:** The repositioning to the end of verse 13 (before the name list) is **firmly supported** by the primary uncial witnesses.

**Manuscript Evidence:**
1. **Codex Sinaiticus (א / 01):** Places the clause directly at the end of verse 13, immediately after the selection of the twelve and before the name Simon. Confirms designation was part of functional commissioning, not a summary added after the list.
2. **Codex Vaticanus (B / 03):** Likewise preserves this sequence. The presence in both earliest 4th-century uncials establishes it as the primitive Lukan/Proto-Lukan reading. Relocation to end of list in later manuscripts is viewed as scribal attempt to provide summary closure.
3. **Tertullian's Testimony:** In *Adversus Marcionem* 4.13.4, addresses the selection of twelve and conferral of "apostle" title as a specific act of power before discussing individual identities—implying the Marcionite Evangelion treated designation as the climax of the selection process.

**Translation of ὠνόμασεν:**
- **"Designated" is correct** over "named" in this forensic/commissioning context
- While derived from ὄνομα (name), the verb here denotes formal conferral of a title, office, or rank
- Jesus is not giving a nickname but legally appointing them to a commission
- Consistent with forensic acts throughout the Jamesian stratum (e.g., renaming Simon to Peter as conferral of authority)

**Structural Logic:**
The Messenger first selects the group → then confers the official rank (emissary) → finally identifies individuals by name. This sequence emphasizes office over individual biological identities.

### Question 3c: The Name List (Luke 6:14-16) - Was it in Proto-Luke? ✅ RESOLVED

> **Audit update (2026-10, batch 1). Superseded.** 6:14 and 6:16 are attested (Tertullian *Marc.* 4.13.6; Epiphanius *Pan.* 42.11.6, sch. 4) and are printed whole; 6:15 is unattested inside an attested list and is printed flagged `attestation: "unattested"` (grey in the PDF), not as ellipses. Ἰσκαριώθ is the NA27 text; it is not a Western or Marcionite reading (NA27 cites Marcion for Ἰσκαριώτην on Holl's emended text of Epiphanius). See `data/apparatus/luke_06.json`.

**Resolution:** The full twelve-name list should be **abbreviated** with ellipses to indicate the forensic gap. Only names explicitly attested in the Marcionite layer are preserved.

**Scholarly Basis:**
- **Tertullian's Testimony (Adv. Marc. 4.13.6):** Specifically attests Simon/Peter (renaming as "act of power") and Judas Iscariot (the betrayer). Tertullian passes over the intermediate names in silence.
- **Forensic Gap:** The middle names (Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew, Thomas, James of Alphaeus, Simon the Zealot) are strictly **unattested** in the Marcionite layer.
- **Evangelist Names Controversy:** Early readers noted names "not written in the Gospel"—forensic analysis of the Adamantius Dialogue clarifies this refers to the Evangelists (Mark, Luke), not the Twelve.

**Corrected Structure:**
- **Verse 3 (Luke 6:14):** "Simon, whom also he named Peter, and Andrew his brother, [and James and John]..."
- **Verse 4 (Luke 6:15):** "..." (forensic gap)
- **Verse 5 (Luke 6:16):** "[and Judas of James] and Judas Iscariot, who became a betrayer."

**Key Notes:**
- **Named Peter (Πέτρον):** Attested by Tertullian as "Work of Power"
- **Betrayer (προδότης):** Verified by Tertullian, essential to narrative arc
- **Iscariot (Ἰσκαριώθ):** Western tradition spelling retained
- Brackets indicate high-probability but unverified names
- Ellipses indicate forensic gap where names are not attested

## Question 4: Terminology for Jesus - "the Messenger" vs "Jesus" vs "the Anointed One" ✅ RESOLVED

**Resolution:** The title "the Messenger" (when referring to Jesus) should be replaced with **"Jesus"** for historical identification and **"the Anointed One"** for his functional title. Lowercase "messenger" translating ἄγγελος is retained.

### Terminology

**Jesus (Ἰησοῦς)** — Historical Identification
- The historically unambiguous name preserved across all strata
- In the Jamesian context, a biological human of dynastic lineage (the Desposyni), not a pneumatic phantom
- The narrative incipit identifies "Jesus" as the specific historical vessel descending into the timeframe of Tiberius and Pilate

**The Anointed One (ὁ χριστός)** — Forensic Title
- Literal translation of Hebrew משיח (Mashiah) and Greek χριστός
- Strips away high-theological debris of "Christ" (which has become a surname in Western narrative)
- Restores the specific Second Temple claim to the throne of David
- The Jerusalem Pillars viewed Jesus as "the Anointed One" whose intercessory authority was continued by James the Just

**messenger (ἄγγελος)** — Lowercase, Retained
- Denotes those sent with a specific legal or divine mandate
- Applies to celestial beings or human envoys (e.g., John's disciples, the seventy)
- Maintains clinical distinction between the Sovereign and his representatives

### Rationale for Displacement of "the Messenger"

- **Removal of Gnostic Vector:** The title echoes Marcionite/Gnostic concept of an "alien" emissary from the Pleroma. In the Jamesian stratum, Jesus is a restorationist "Opposition High Priest" rooted in the material world and Torah.
- **Philological Consistency:** The Greek uses Ἰησοῦς as name and ὁ χριστός as title. "The Messenger" prioritizes an interpretive framework over manuscript facts.
- **Hierarchical Clarity:** Calling Jesus "the Messenger" confuses his identity with the emissaries (ἀποστόλους) and messengers (ἀγγέλους) he himself commissioned.

### Status: IMPLEMENTED (audit, 2026-10-12)

- "the Messenger" does not occur in `data/proto_luke.json`; lowercase "messenger" is kept for ἄγγελος where the context is a human or angelic envoy, "angels" elsewhere.
- ὁ χριστός is translated "the Anointed One" in every verse where it occurs (4:41; 9:20; 20:41; 22:67; 23:2; 24:26), in the batch build scripts so that rebuilds keep it.
- The historical claims in the rationale above (Desposyni, "Opposition High Priest") are the library's, not evidence used in the reconstruction; the notes do not repeat them.
- The superseded v1 drafts in `data/reconstructions/` are not maintained and may still contain the old terms.

## Question 5: "Soma Pneumatikon" - Pauline Terminology in a Jamesian Text? ✅ RESOLVED

**Resolution:** The Pauline term "Soma Pneumatikon" should be replaced with Jamesian terminology: **"The Living One"** (ὁ ζῶν) for the post-mortal Jesus, and **"Exaltation"** for the enthronement event.

### Reconstructed Terminology

| Jamesian Term | Greek/Hebrew | Forensic Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **The Living One** | ὁ ζῶν (*ho zōn*) | Title for one vindicated and existing in the pneumatic realm |
| **Appeared** | ὤφθη (*ōphthē*) | Standard forensic term for visionary perception |
| **Exalted/Exaltation** | *Exaltatio* | Enthronement at the "Right Hand of Power" |
| **Spirit** | πνεῦμα (*pneuma*) | The witnesses' default classification (non-corporeal transport) |

### Rationale for Displacement

- **Davidic Necrolatry:** The Jerusalem Church preserved Jesus's tomb (Talpiot) as dynastic memory. Paul's "spiritual body" replacing the physical shell was alien to this community.
- **Translation Fable:** The Jamesian community viewed post-mortem events through prophetic translation (Elijah, Moses)—exaltation to heaven, not medical resurrection.
- **Philological Honesty:** "The Living One" and "Exaltation" restore the prophetic vocabulary of the "Assembly of the Hebrews."

### Status: IMPLEMENTED (audit, 2026-10-12)

- No "Soma Pneumatikon" or "spiritual body" language occurs in the reconstruction; the batch scripts check for it.
- ὁ ζῶν at 24:5 is translated "the Living One", and the chapter 22 notes use the term.

The text keeps "a spirit does not have bones as you see that I have" (24:39) and the request for food (24:41); see `docs/audit/AUDIT_LOG.md`, batch 6, and `docs/audit/library_corrections.md` item 13.

## Question 6: Preface Text - Forensic Methodology and Witnesses ✅ RESOLVED

**Resolution:** The preface should be rewritten to reflect the scholarly grounding of the reconstruction.

### I. Marcionite Priority and Editorial Fatigue

The "New Marcionite Paradigm" posits that the *Evangelion* represents a pre-canonical stratum of the Lukan tradition.

**Key Scholarly Sources:**
- **Matthias Klinghardt**, *The Oldest Gospel and the Formation of the Canonical Gospels* (2015/2021): Identifies editorial fatigue as primary proof—canonical Luke exhibits fatigue when diverging from the Marcionite text.
- **Jason BeDuhn**, *The First New Testament: Marcion's Scriptural Canon* (2013): Demonstrates that differences are absences of material not yet added, not Marcionite excisions.
- **Markus Vinzent**, *Christ's Resurrection in Early Christianity* (2011): Argues Marcion's shorter resurrection text was the baseline later "materialized" by orthodox editors.

**Example:** The Capernaum fatigue (Luke 4:23)—Jesus references Capernaum miracles before visiting Capernaum in canonical order.

### II. The "Orthodox Wrapper"

**Note:** "Orthodox Wrapper" is a project-specific coinage for transparency. Scholarly equivalents:
- **"Pastoral Prologue"**: Luke 1–2 (infancy narratives, Temple piety, Davidic biology)
- **"Orthodox Redaction"**: Later additions to domesticate the pneumatic Jesus into biological/political framework

### III. Western Non-Interpolations

Term coined by **Westcott and Hort** (19th c.) for passages present in Alexandrian manuscripts but absent in Western tradition (Codex Bezae, Old Latin).

**Key Passages Excised:**
- Luke 24:3: "of the Lord Jesus"
- Luke 24:6: "He is not here, but has been raised"
- Luke 24:12: Peter's visit to tomb
- Luke 24:39-43: Physicalist proofs (flesh, bones, fish)
- Luke 24:51: "carried up into heaven"

These restore a narrative where the empty tomb is a sign of **absence and perplexity**, not forensic proof of resuscitation.

## Question 7: The petition for the holy spirit in the Lord's Prayer (Luke 11:2) ✅ RESOLVED

**Question.** Tertullian (*Marc.* 4.26.3–5) shows that the prayer in this text asked for the holy spirit. Which petition did it replace, and in what words?

**Evidence.**
- Tertullian turns each petition into a question, in the order of the prayer, and then says "in this order" (*hoc ordine*): Father; "From whom shall I ask the holy spirit?"; "Whose kingdom shall I wish to come?"; bread; sins; temptation. There is no question about the hallowing of the name. The spirit petition therefore stood where canonical Luke has "hallowed be your name", and "your kingdom come" was kept.
- The only Greek witnesses for a spirit petition in Luke's prayer (minuscules 700, 162; Gregory of Nyssa; Maximus) read ἐλθέτω τὸ πνεῦμά σου τὸ ἅγιον ἐφ' ἡμᾶς καὶ καθαρισάτω ἡμᾶς. In them it replaces "your kingdom come". NA27 cites (McionT) for it.
- Tertullian gives no wording. Roth (2015, ch. 4.4.45) rates the spirit very likely, and the wording unknown.

**Decision.** The spirit petition is printed in place of "hallowed be your name", as ἐλθέτω τὸ πνεῦμά σου τὸ ἅγιον ("may your holy spirit come"). This is the verb and order of the Greek manuscript form, limited to the elements Tertullian attests. "Upon us and cleanse us" is not printed. "Your kingdom come" is kept. The apparatus (`data/apparatus/luke_11.json`) and the chapter 9 note mark the wording as unattested.

**Status.** Implemented in batch 3 (chapter 9).

## Question 8: Do the parables Marcion's text lacks carry a correspondential composition that the attested parables do not? — OPEN

**Observation (owner).** Some parables that are unattested for this text resolve fully under Swedenborg's doctrine of correspondences, as compositions in which each element has a fixed spiritual sense. This looks like a way of writing that the author of the attested text did not use.

**Evidence so far.** The audit gives the parables three groups (apparatus classes, batches 2–5):

| Group | Parables |
|---|---|
| Absent or probably absent | Good Samaritan (10:29–37, U−); barren fig tree (13:6–9, A−); two sons (15:11–32, A−); tenants (20:9–18, A− / U−) |
| Unattested, not printed | tower and king at war (14:28–33); unworthy servants (17:7–10) |
| Attested present | sower (8:4–8); friend at midnight (11:5–8); rich fool (12:16–21); mustard seed and leaven (13:18–21); great banquet (14:16–24); lost sheep and coin (15:3–10); unjust steward (16:1–8); rich man and Lazarus (16:19–31); unjust judge (18:1–8); Pharisee and tax collector (18:9–14); minas (19:11–27); fig tree in leaf (21:29–31) |

Related findings:
- The stylometric baseline (`docs/stylometry/REPORT.md` §5.3) found one signal only: an excess of Luke's characteristic vocabulary in unattested material (ῥῆμα, πίμπλημι, λαός, πνεῦμα, ἄγγελος). The signal is lexical, not grammatical.
- The library's "correspondential depth test" (*Swedenborg's Key and the Proto-Lukan Hypothesis* §2.1; *No Original Exists* §6.4; *The River and the Vessel* §8.3) uses the Samaritan and the Prodigal as Proto-Lukan examples. Both are in the first group (`docs/audit/library_corrections.md`, item 8).

**Question.** Is correspondential completeness distributed unevenly across the three groups? If it is concentrated in the absent parables, correspondential composition marks material that the Marcionite text lacked, rather than its earliest stratum. It would then be a compositional signature of a second hand or a later layer.

**Design (to be fixed before scoring).**
1. **Criteria.** Fix the scoring criteria in advance from the library's own method of correspondential reading. Proposed measures, per parable:
   - the share of its narrative elements (persons, objects, places, actions, numbers) that have an established correspondence;
   - whether those senses cohere into one reading of the whole.
   Sources: *A Library of Biblical Correspondences in Nature and Objects*; Swedenborg, *Arcana Coelestia*, cited directly.
2. **Blind scoring.** Score each parable from its text with the attestation class withheld. The parables are prepared as neutral, unlabeled texts, and the scorer does not consult the apparatus.
3. **Comparison.** Only then compare the groups (absent vs. attested; a rank test, given the small numbers).
4. **Reach.** With about six against twelve parables, the result can be suggestive, not decisive. Record it as such.
5. **Controls.** Score a few parables from Matthew and Mark as controls (e.g. the wicked tenants in Mark 12, the talents in Matthew 25). They show whether the signature is Lukan or synoptic.

**Protocol, fixed before any data were extracted (2026-10-13).** Swedenborg is used as the rater. He wrote a century before Marcion's text was reconstructed, so his treatment of a parable cannot reflect its attestation class. This replaces the blind-scoring design above as the primary measure; the blind scoring remains a possible second measure.

1. **Corpus.** *Arcana Coelestia* (Standard Edition, 12 vols, 1949–54) and *Apocalypse Explained* (1946, 6 vols), from the OCR text layers on the Internet Archive. These are Swedenborg's two continuous exegetical works.
2. **Parables.**
   - The 18 Lukan parables listed above, each taken with its synoptic parallels (Swedenborg often cites Matthew's form). The parallels are fixed before extraction, from the parallel references printed in NA27 (only those listed there).
   - Controls: Matthew 13 (wheat and tares; treasure; pearl; net), Matthew 20:1–16 (labourers), Matthew 25:1–13 (ten virgins).
3. **Elements.** For each parable, the element inventory is the set of concrete nouns in its KJV text: persons, animals, plants, objects, places, numbers. It is drawn up mechanically from the text before extraction, and frozen.
4. **Citing passages.** Every numbered section of the corpus that cites a verse of the parable or its parallels, by book, chapter and verse ("Luke x. 30", "Luke 10:30", "Matt. xiii. 24" and the OCR variants of these).
5. **Decoded element.** An element counts as decoded if a citing section states a sense for it. The test is the element's noun within 25 words of *signifies, signified, denotes, is meant, are meant, means, represents, corresponds*.
6. **Primary measure.** The share of a parable's elements that are decoded anywhere in the corpus.
   - Secondary measures: the number of citing sections, and the number of decoded elements.
7. **Comparison.** Absent and probably-absent parables (4) against attested parables (12): Mann–Whitney test, one-sided in the direction of the observation (absent > attested), plus the two group medians. The unattested pair (2) and the controls (6) are reported separately.
8. **Reach.** With 4 against 12 parables, only a large difference can be shown, and the result is recorded as suggestive. Swedenborg's attention also depends on things other than composition: Matthew's parallels, and doctrinal use. Both are reported with the result.

**Precision check on that measure.** 30 decoded-element hits were drawn at random (seed 20261013) and read in context, with the parable name withheld. About 10 of 30 are a statement of the element's sense in connection with the parable or in general (e.g. "the piece of silver signifies truth"; "he that laid up the pound in a napkin denotes…"). The rest are noise:
- tagging errors ("thou", "out", "forth", "well", "one");
- generic nouns ("man", "house", "father", "place") matched in unrelated scripture quoted in the same long section;
- numbers.

The measure as fixed does not measure what it was meant to. The first result is therefore not evidence either way.

**Revised measure (fixed 2026-10-13, after the precision check and before running it).**
- **Window.** From 300 words before to 300 words after each reference to a verse of the parable or its parallels, not the whole section. Swedenborg quotes the passage and expounds it next to the reference.
- **Decoded.** The element's word stands within 6 words *before* a signification verb (signifies, signify, signified, denotes, denote, denoted, means, mean, is/are meant, represents, represent, corresponds, correspond), or in the construction "by (the) X is/are meant/signified/denoted".
- **Inventory.** As before, without cardinal numbers, without WordNet noun.quantity, and without closed-class words mis-tagged as nouns (thou, thee, ye, one, out, forth, well).
- **Precision check.** The same check is repeated on 30 new hits before the result is read.

**Editions actually used.** The 1940s editions named in item 1 are lending-restricted on the Internet Archive. The public-domain editions were used instead: *Arcana Coelestia* (1870 and 1915 English editions; where both are present, the first copy of a section is kept) and *Apocalypse Explained* (1890, 6 vols).

**Parser correction (2026-10-13, before the revised run).** The first parser read a bare reference ("xv. 22, 23") only inside parentheses. The 1870 AC prints such references without them, so most AC citations were missed. For example, AC 9391 quotes Luke 15:22–23 and expounds the robe, the ring, the shoes and the calf. A bare reference now belongs to the nearest book named before it. Both measures were rerun on the corrected parser; the figures below are those reruns.

**Descriptive, chosen after seeing the data (not a test).** The largest number of a parable's elements decoded in one section:

If this threshold had been fixed in advance, the probability of the split (4/4 against 5/12) would be 0.07. It was not fixed in advance.

**What the data show.**
1. The observation that prompted Q8 holds as a description. Swedenborg expounds each of the four parables Marcion's text lacks, element by element. The passages are AE 375 and 444 (Samaritan), AE 403 (barren fig tree), AC 9391 (prodigal son) and AC 1069 (tenants).
2. The absent group scores higher than the attested group on both measures, but not significantly (p ≈ 0.15; n = 4 against 12). The attested parables and the Matthean controls are decoded at similar rates. On these data, the correspondential reading does not separate the two layers of the text.
3. The design has a limit that more data would not remove. Swedenborg treats the whole canonical text as the Word and decodes whatever he cites. His rate for a parable therefore measures how often he cited it: across the 24 parables, share decoded correlates with the number of citing sections (Spearman ρ = 0.44, p = 0.03). What he decodes is shaped by what he chose to cite. It does not show whether the text was written correspondentially.
4. A test of whether the Lukan author composed correspondentially would have to measure the text itself, not a reader's treatment of it. An example is the density, per parable and by attestation class, of the nouns Swedenborg's dictionary of correspondences assigns a fixed sense, with the dictionary fixed before the parables are scored. This is open, if wanted.
